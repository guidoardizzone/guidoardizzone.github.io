Working Papers

Senior Debt and Market Access

with Agustín Velasquez (IMF) and Mahamoud Islam (IMF) · Presented at the IMF Fund Internship Program conference, 2026

Abstract

Multilateral creditors now account for nearly 40% of external public debt in emerging market and developing economies. While their effective seniority may improve market confidence through liquidity and policy support, it may also potentially dilute the claims of private creditors. This paper examines how multilateral debt exposure is associated with the pricing of sovereign private external debt. Using a panel of 56 EMDEs over 2000–2024 that combines JP Morgan EMBIG spreads with data from the World Bank's International Debt Statistics (IDS), we estimate a spread equation allowing for a nonlinear effect of the multilateral debt share. We find a robust non-monotonic relationship: increases in multilateral exposure are initially associated with lower spreads, but the relationship reverses once the multilateral share exceeds about 56%. The reversal is strongest among more vulnerable sovereigns, characterized by high public and external debt, and elevated spreads. We interpret these findings as a conditional association consistent with a catalytic-then-dilutive view of official lending rather than as causal evidence.

Sanctions, Lending, and the Bank Ownership Channel

with Matteo Ficarra

Abstract

We study how economic sanctions affect domestic bank lending using a panel of 1,886 banks in 56 emerging market economies over 1995–2021. Foreign-owned banks reduce loan growth by 12.8 percentage points more than domestic private banks when their host country is sanctioned; state-owned banks show no differential response. A funding squeeze is behind the lower credit. When we decompose by sanction type, the effect concentrates in episodes where financial sanctions are active. The contraction is not driven by bilateral legal compliance: foreign banks whose parent country formally participates in the sanctions coalition do not cut lending significantly more than other foreign banks. Instead, the response is concentrated among foreign banks whose parent countries are geopolitically aligned with the United States; US-aligned foreign banks reduce lending by 14.7 percentage points more than domestic private banks, while non-aligned foreign banks show no differential response. These results identify integration into the US dollar payment network as the operative channel through which sanctions compress domestic credit.
